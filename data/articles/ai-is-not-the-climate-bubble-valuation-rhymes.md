:::heading
text: The wrong question is whether AI is a bubble
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The market wants a clean label: AI bubble or not AI bubble. That is too crude. The current AI cycle is not the same as the 2020-21 clean-energy run, and it is not a simple replay of dot-com either. The first-wave AI winners have real numbers. NVIDIA is not a concept stock. Microsoft, Meta, and Alphabet are not waiting for a policy subsidy to discover revenue. Broadcom and TSMC are tied to genuine semiconductor and packaging demand. If the comparison stops at business quality, AI is clearly stronger than the climate-tech boom."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "But market cycles rarely break at the first-wave winner. They break when the theme spreads outward and every adjacent company starts being valued as if it owns the bottleneck. That is where AI is beginning to look familiar. The initial trade was compute. Then semiconductors. Then networking. Then data centers. Then power equipment. Then cooling. Then memory. Then software agents. Eventually the market stops asking whether a company has durable economics and starts asking only whether it has enough thematic exposure to justify a higher multiple. That is the climate-tech rhyme."
:::

:::heading
text: AI has better earnings than climate-tech had
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The strongest argument against the bubble label is simple: the leaders are making money now. In the 2026-06-04 yfinance snapshot, NVIDIA had a market cap of roughly $5.2 trillion, trailing revenue of about $253.5 billion, a price-to-sales multiple around 20.5x, a forward P/E around 17.0x, and an operating margin around 65.6%. Microsoft traded around 10.0x sales and 22.1x forward earnings with a 46.3% operating margin. Meta was around 7.4x sales and 17.2x forward earnings with a 40.6% operating margin. Those are expensive companies, but they are not science projects."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "That is the biggest difference from climate-tech. The clean-energy run had a powerful macro story: decarbonization, electrification, ESG flows, solar adoption, hydrogen optionality, and policy support. Some companies were real. But the public-market basket included many businesses where profits, project economics, supply discipline, and financing durability were not yet proven. When rates rose, supply chains tightened, and capital became less free, the theme did not disappear — the valuations did. ICLN rose about 163% from January 2020 to its February 2021 peak, then remained roughly 21% below that peak in the latest yfinance check. TAN rose about 282% into its 2021 peak and was still about 42% below that peak. The theme was real. The entry price was not forgiving."
:::

:::heading
text: The rhyme is valuation front-running
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The current AI tape shows the same front-running pattern. The market first paid for the obvious bottleneck: NVIDIA. Then it paid for the broader semiconductor chain. The SMH semiconductor ETF was up about 474% from the ChatGPT launch date to 2026-06-03. That is not inherently irrational; AI demand has genuinely pulled forward chip, memory, networking, power, and cooling spend. The problem is that each outward step lowers the purity of exposure while often keeping the valuation premium."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "This is why the AI market should be split into buckets. There are real expensive names, where high multiples are backed by high margins and dominant positioning. NVIDIA, Broadcom, TSMC, Microsoft, Meta, and Alphabet belong somewhere in that debate. Then there are narrative expensive names, where the business may be strong but the valuation already assumes near-perfect conversion of AI demand into long-term economics. Palantir at roughly 65.3x sales and 68.6x forward earnings is not the same risk as Meta at roughly 7.4x sales and 17.2x forward earnings. AMD at roughly 23.6x sales and 41.7x forward earnings is not being valued like a normal semiconductor company; it is being valued like an AI acceleration catch-up must happen. Vertiv and Eaton are real AI infrastructure beneficiaries, but after large reratings, they now require a more disciplined entry-price conversation."
:::

:::heading
text: Climate-tech is the useful warning, not the perfect analogy
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The climate analogy fails if it implies AI is fake. AI is already showing up in cloud capex, GPU shortages, inference workloads, model deployment, data-center power demand, and enterprise software budgets. The analogy works only at the level of market psychology. In both cycles, investors moved from structural demand to valuation extrapolation. In both cycles, the supply chain created genuine bottlenecks. In both cycles, capital rushed toward companies that could claim proximity to the bottleneck. And in both cycles, the danger was not being early to the theme — it was being late to the multiple expansion."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The clean-energy run also reminds us that physical infrastructure themes are not software curves. They face permitting, interconnection, financing, equipment lead times, commodity costs, customer concentration, and regulatory risk. AI infrastructure has its own version of this: power availability, grid queues, advanced packaging, HBM supply, cooling conversion, depreciation, and hyperscaler capex discipline. If those constraints tighten, they can support winners. If they slow deployment or pressure margins, they can punish any stock priced as if capacity converts cleanly into free cash flow."
:::

:::heading
text: The practical investor rule
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The right conclusion is not to short AI or chase every AI dip. It is to stop treating AI exposure as a single quality label. A company can be part of a real structural boom and still be a bad stock at the wrong price. Climate-tech taught that lesson brutally. The market did not stop caring about decarbonization; it stopped paying fantasy multiples for unproven economics once capital costs changed."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "For AI, the discipline is straightforward. Own the profitable bottlenecks when valuation still allows underwriting. Be more careful with companies whose AI story is second-order, crowded, or dependent on perfect demand pass-through. Watch for multiple expansion moving faster than evidence. And do not pay monopoly valuation for non-monopoly economics. AI is a better business cycle than climate-tech was. That is exactly why the valuation mistakes will be more tempting."
:::

:::verdict
label: Bottom line
text: "AI is not the climate bubble at the fundamental level. The leading AI companies have real earnings power that most climate-tech winners never had. But the valuation pattern is starting to rhyme: first the market pays for the bottleneck, then it pays for every company near the bottleneck, and eventually it stops distinguishing structural winner from narrative passenger. The investment edge is not deciding whether AI is real. It is deciding which AI valuations still leave room for reality to be merely good rather than perfect."
:::
