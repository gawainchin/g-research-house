:::thesis-card
label: Core Thesis
title: The cleaner AI-infrastructure equity is the one with the tighter power-to-financing loop.
text: "The market still talks about AI infrastructure as a demand story. The sharper filter is whether a company can turn secured power into financeable, deliverable compute without blowing up margin quality. On that test, IREN currently looks structurally safer than Nebius: the growth is slower, but the contract structure, prepayment support, and capex burden are cleaner. Nebius may still win big, but it is asking investors to underwrite more things at once. This is a structural-quality comparison, not a live valuation call; if the market has already priced in IREN’s cleaner setup, the better business structure does not automatically mean the better stock entry."
:::

:::heading
text: The real moat is not demand visibility
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The easy AI-infrastructure trade is to treat every fast-growing neocloud as a pure demand beneficiary. That misses the harder part of the business. Demand matters, but the equity outcome is usually decided one layer lower: who can secure power, finance the build, deliver the capacity on time, and still preserve acceptable unit economics once depreciation and power costs show up. That framing currently favors IREN over Nebius."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "IREN's case is cleaner because the loop is tighter. Its November 2025 Microsoft agreement covers roughly $9.7 billion over five years, includes a 20% prepayment, and is tied to phased GB300 deployment through 2026 at Childress. Reuters reported that the prepayment helps finance part of IREN's roughly $5.8 billion Dell equipment purchase. By May 2026, IREN said Horizon 1-4 remained on track, 480MW of 2026 capacity was on track and fully contracted, and contracted ARR had reached $3.1 billion. That does not eliminate execution risk, but it means the company has a clearer customer-backed path from power to cash generation. The comparison is deliberately uneven: IREN’s evidence is contract/funding visibility, while Nebius’s evidence is faster current revenue scale but less proven terminal economics." 
:::

:::heading
text: Nebius is growing faster, but with more moving parts
:::

:::paragraph
text: "Nebius is obviously the higher-velocity story. Q1 2026 revenue jumped to $399.0 million from $50.9 million, AI cloud revenue reached $389.7 million, ARR rose to $1.92 billion, and adjusted EBITDA turned positive at $129.5 million. On the surface, that looks like proof the model is already working. The problem is that the accounting quality underneath is still mixed. Operating loss remained negative at $128.0 million. Depreciation and amortization exploded to $212.0 million. Adjusted net loss widened to $100.3 million. Reported net income of $621.2 million was heavily helped by a $780.6 million non-cash gain from equity revaluation. In other words, the revenue machine is real, but the durable earnings machine is still under construction."
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The financing structure also asks for more trust. Nebius's September 2025 Microsoft 6-K explicitly said the parties' obligations begin only after Nebius confirms it has secured the additional financing required for the capital expenditures under the agreement. Since then the company has executed impressively, but it has also had to raise enormous capital: $4.3375 billion of converts, $2.0 billion of prefunded warrants, a $2.0 billion NVIDIA investment, and then roughly $2.47 billion of property-and-equipment spending in Q1 alone. That is not inherently bad. It just means the business is still being financed like a scaling buildout, not yet harvested like a mature infrastructure utility." 
:::

:::heading
text: The Bloom debate is about economics clarity, not doom
:::

:::paragraph
text: "The online hot take is that Nebius's Bloom fuel-cell arrangement proves hyperscaler deals are uneconomic. That overstates the evidence. What the release clearly shows is that Nebius wants faster time-to-power and cleaner permitting at a site where 328MW of installed capacity is expected this year. What remains unclear is how much that power costs on a fully loaded basis and how much of that cost can be passed through to customers. The widely repeated '$2.6 billion' figure appears to be an aggregate maximum service-fee number over the term, not a clean annual economics disclosure. Without a stated denominator, using it as proof of structural unprofitability is sloppy. If one assumes a 10-year term, the rough sensitivity is about $1.04 million per guaranteed MW per year on 250MW, or about $0.79 million per installed MW per year on 328MW — useful context, but still not a disclosed all-in margin answer." 
:::

:::paragraph
text: "Still, the Bloom arrangement does reinforce the broader caution on Nebius. Behind-the-meter fuel cells, giant capex, financing layers, and hyperscaler commitments can absolutely work. But they create more variables between revenue growth and durable free cash flow. IREN's model currently has fewer of those variables because its customer prepayment, narrower scope, and contracted 2026 capacity make the funding chain easier to follow."
:::

:::verdict
label: Bottom line
text: "IREN looks structurally safer than Nebius today not because Nebius is broken, but because IREN's power-to-financing-to-delivery chain is easier to underwrite. Nebius may end up the bigger winner if it proves that its scale can carry through to durable EBIT and free cash flow. Right now, though, IREN is the cleaner evidence-backed expression, while Nebius remains the higher-beta execution story with more to prove. Treat that as a structure-and-risk judgment first; a buy/sell decision still needs current valuation, position sizing, and entry discipline."
:::
